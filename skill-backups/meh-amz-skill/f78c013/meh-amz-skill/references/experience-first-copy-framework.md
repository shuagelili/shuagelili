# Experience-First Listing Framework

Use this reference whenever the product has a meaningful usage scene, task, ritual, emotional outcome, or visible before/after state. The purpose is to turn verified product facts into customer-relevant shopping experiences without inventing benefits.

## Core model

Do not begin with a feature list. Begin with the buyer's situation:

```text
shopping scene -> buyer problem or desire -> product mechanism -> completed task -> concrete outcome
```

For every retained scene, record:

- who is using or buying the product;
- when or where the scene occurs;
- what problem, desire, or task starts the interaction;
- which verified product fact performs the work;
- what observable result the buyer gets;
- which evidence and customer-facing fields support the scene.

The outcome must be concrete and grounded. Prefer “keeps cables separated on a desk” over “improves your life”; prefer “creates a 24-day Christmas countdown activity” over “brings unlimited joy”.

## Experience modes

Select one primary mode and, when supported by verified facts, one or two secondary modes. Do not force a festive narrative onto an ordinary product.

| Mode | Typical trigger | Product mechanism | Customer outcome |
|---|---|---|---|
| `festive_ritual` | buyer wants a holiday countdown, celebration, or shared tradition | daily reveal, seasonal sequence, themed contents, displayable result | a repeatable holiday ritual and a visible seasonal result |
| `task_completion` | buyer needs to complete a practical task | tool geometry, capacity, compartments, adjustment, workflow support | the task is finished with fewer steps or less disorder |
| `problem_solving` | buyer is frustrated by a recurring inconvenience | specific feature directly addresses the pain point | less searching, spilling, bending, clutter, or repeated handling |
| `daily_comfort` | buyer wants a more comfortable repeated routine | material, surface, fit, posture, grip, light, or sound characteristics | a more comfortable and predictable daily interaction |
| `display_and_atmosphere` | buyer wants an object to integrate into a room or occasion | form, size, theme, finish, assembly result | a clear decorative or atmosphere contribution |
| `exploration_and_learning` | buyer wants discovery, practice, or shared learning | progressive steps, challenge, educational structure, open-ended use | an understandable learning or exploration outcome |
| `portable_or_outdoor_use` | buyer must use, carry, or protect something away from home | packability, closure, resistance, weight, access, weather-related feature | easier transport or use in the named environment |

`festive_ritual` is appropriate for Advent calendars and seasonal products. For storage, tools, office items, kitchenware, beauty, furniture, and similar products, start with `task_completion`, `problem_solving`, `daily_comfort`, `display_and_atmosphere`, or `portable_or_outdoor_use`.

## Scene coverage rules

Use at least three distinct verified scenes when the product supports them. A scene is distinct only when the audience, trigger, task, or expected outcome changes. Three versions of “use at home” are one scene, not three.

Good scene differences include:

- daily work vs. travel;
- sorting vs. retrieving;
- family activity vs. individual hobby;
- assembly vs. finished display;
- preparation vs. cleanup;
- gifting vs. personal use.

Map the scenes across the record rather than repeating one scene in every field:

- title: product noun plus primary scene or outcome;
- Highlights: compact specification or secondary outcome not already in the title;
- bullets: one scene and one primary benefit per bullet;
- description: buyer journey from trigger to completed outcome;
- use guide: actions in the order the buyer performs them;
- frontend keywords: customer language for the scene and product noun;
- backend terms: relevant vocabulary not naturally used in visible copy;
- images: visual proof of trigger, mechanism, result, size, contents, and limitations;
- A+: a short sequence showing the product in use and then in its completed state.

## Five-bullet architecture

Keep the existing exact validator format: `【UPPERCASE HEADER】 : content`. Use this semantic pattern for each bullet:

```text
【SCENE OR PAIN-POINT】 : buyer situation + verified mechanism + concrete outcome.
```

Recommended five-bullet coverage:

1. primary task, ritual, or use case;
2. main product function or contents;
3. material, construction, fit, or handling experience;
4. secondary scene, audience, or visual/detail outcome;
5. size, capacity, compatibility, care, limitation, or display result.

Change the order when the category has a stronger buyer sequence, but keep one main benefit per bullet. Do not use generic headers such as “HIGH QUALITY”.

For festive products, the first bullet can sell the ritual. For ordinary products, replace the ritual with the task or pain point. For example:

- festive: daily opening -> progressive assembly -> completed seasonal scene;
- storage: cluttered drawer -> separated compartments -> faster retrieval;
- phone stand: prolonged hand holding -> adjustable support -> more comfortable viewing;
- kitchen tool: repeated preparation step -> dedicated mechanism -> quicker, cleaner handling.

## Description architecture

The description should follow the buyer journey instead of repeating the bullets:

1. What is the product and what scene does it belong to?
2. Who uses or receives it?
3. What action does the buyer perform first?
4. Which verified mechanism or contents support the task?
5. What is the completed result or after-use state?
6. What should the buyer know about limits, care, safety, size, compatibility, or storage?

Q&A headings are useful for voice and visual scanning, but the answer must remain specific and evidence-backed. Keep internal review language, evidence labels, unresolved gaps, and hypotheses outside the customer-visible description.

## Images and A+ mapping

Plan images as proof of the experience, not decoration alone:

1. product noun and main use scene;
2. contents, capacity, or sequence;
3. the mechanism in action;
4. material, fit, texture, or handling detail;
5. completed result or display state;
6. dimensions, compatibility, age, safety, or limitations;
7. secondary real-life scene.

The exact slot order may change by category, but every major customer claim should have a visual proof slot or a clear reason why an image cannot prove it. A+ modules should move from trigger to mechanism to result and should not add claims absent from the listing record.

## Evidence and language controls

Experience framing never upgrades an unverified feature into a fact. Each scene must link to own-product facts and evidence. Competitor observations can inspire scene selection, vocabulary, or gap analysis, but cannot authorize customer copy unless the user separately confirms the same attribute for the own product.

Avoid unsupported emotional or performance claims such as “perfect”, “premium”, “guaranteed”, “effortless”, “unlimited”, “improves your life”, or “built to last”. Replace them with observable outcomes tied to a mechanism, such as “24 compartments”, “fits a 25 x 23 x 5 cm display area”, “keeps items separated”, or “assembled scene for indoor display”.

## Review questions

Before validation, ask:

- Can a buyer recognize the situation in the first sentence?
- Does every claimed outcome have a product mechanism behind it?
- Are at least three scenes genuinely different when the product supports them?
- Does each bullet have one primary benefit and a distinct header?
- Does the description explain the before/use/after sequence rather than repeat the bullets?
- Do images prove the scene, mechanism, result, and limits?
- Are all emotional, durability, ease, and quality phrases grounded in evidence?
